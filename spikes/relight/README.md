# Relighting spike (ADR 0018, step 2)

A throwaway comparison page for the gate in [ADR 0018](../../docs/decisions/0018-two-tier-scene-renderer.md):
**does sun relighting (WebGL2) read clearly better than blended keyframes (Canvas 2D)?**
Nothing here ships; `npm run build` does not include it. If the gate passes, the tiers are rebuilt
properly in `src/` (steps 3 and 4) and this directory is deleted.

## Running it

```sh
npm run spike:relight            # http://localhost:5173 on this machine
npm run spike:relight -- --host  # also on the local network, e.g. for the Surface Pro
```

`--host` exposes the development server to the local network while it runs; stop it afterwards.
Use a real GPU browser (Chrome or Edge). Headless screenshots need `?softgl=1`, which accepts
software WebGL; never use it for measurements.

Both halves draw the same scene from the same `LightingState` (`src/environment/`), with the same
sky, stars, Sun, Moon, and ground shadows. Only the shading of the art differs:

- **Relit · WebGL2** (left): every texel lit from its albedo and normal by the real Sun and Moon,
  every frame.
- **Keyframed · Canvas 2D** (right): the same shading, run once per layer for four keyframes
  authored on the March equinox (dawn, noon, dusk, full-moon night). At runtime they are blended by
  Sun altitude and side, darkened to the current light level, and cached; the cache is rebuilt only
  when the blend changes.

Controls: split/relit/keyframed (`1` `2` `3`, `X` flips full screen), sky previews or a custom
date and time in the Blue Ridge (`←` `→` ±10 minutes, `Space` plays a day in 48 seconds), normals
derived by script or generated by Codex (`N`), light from the real direction or from the viewer's
side (`L`), large sprites (`I`), and a normal-map or flat-albedo view of the relit half. `H` hides
the panel. Every setting is also a URL parameter (`view`, `sky`, `date`, `minutes`, `normals`,
`side`, `inspect`, `mode`, `ui=0`).

## Maintainer decision (2026-09-27) and the tuning round

**Relit (WebGL2) with Codex-generated normal maps.** Performance was fine in both tiers, even on
a first-generation Surface Pro. The page now opens relit with Codex normals; `?compare=1` brings
back the keyframed half and the derived normals.

Light stays physical (a southern Sun backlights the scene), with two terms standing in for what
ray tracing would give:

- **Translucency** (slider, default 100%): light passing through grass (0.6), petals and leaves
  (0.9) when lit from behind.
- **Front fill** (slider, default 40%, `[` `]` ±5%): light bounced back from the field, as the Sun
  and Moon mirrored to the viewer's side at that fraction of their power. It reaches the hill and
  the plants only; a fill near the viewer would not visibly brighten the mountains.

Open: the maintainer picks the fill power (and confirms translucency) to carry into the GPU tier.

## What was judged in the first round

1. **Relit vs keyframed** at dawn, noon, dusk, and night (the Sunrise, Noon, Sunset, and Full
   moon previews), and while playing a day. Keyframes cannot know the season or where the Sun is,
   so differences grow away from the equinox (try December and June) and at the hours between
   keyframes.
2. **Codex vs derived normals**, especially on the large sprites and the hill.
3. **Light direction.** In the Blue Ridge the Sun and Moon are always to the south, in front of a
   south-facing viewer, so real light backlights the scene most of the day, and a moonlit night is
   nearly black. "Viewer's side" mirrors the light to the front, as on a stage. This is an art
   direction choice independent of the tier.
4. **Frame cost** on the dedicated monitor and the Surface Pro: the stats line shows the draw time
   of each tier, the software tier's rebuild time, and frames per second while playing. Measure
   with one tier showing (`2` or `3`), not split.

## Findings so far (before the maintainer's review)

- **Flat-lit albedo works.** Asked for "perfectly even, shadowless, directionless lighting," the
  image tool returned sprites and layers without painted light direction. Transparent layers (sky
  keyed out above the ridges and hill) also work.
- **Sizes are capped.** Every image came back at 1254×1254 or 1672×941, as in P2-A, whatever was
  requested; backdrop layers are upscaled to 1920×1080. Native 1080p-plus layers need tiling or
  upscaling with review.
- **Alpha is noisy.** Transparency is keyed from a solid color: interiors sit near alpha 250,
  and stray texels of alpha 1 carry the key color (pure green on the hill). The loader snaps alpha
  below 8 to 0 and above 239 to 255, and textures are premultiplied, so no key color bleeds.
- **Codex normal maps are plausible but not dependable.** Sprite normals line up with the albedo
  and read well (the berry is a sphere, petals cup, veins rise), but came back without alpha, with
  a checkerboard baked in; the albedo's silhouette is used instead. The ridge map follows the
  OpenGL convention; the **hill map has its X axis inverted**, corrected by `flipX` in
  `main.ts`. A production pipeline must check the convention of every generated map.
- **Derived normals need an exact distance transform.** A chamfer transform turned the jagged
  crest into streaks down the layer; an exact Euclidean transform plus smoothing fixed it. Derived
  normals only round a layer's silhouette, so all three ridges in one image get one rounded top
  edge: separate layers per ridge would light better.
- **Canvas `multiply` tints bleed into soft edges** (a pale line along every crest), so the
  software tier darkens with `source-atop` black by a brightness-only factor instead; the
  keyframes already carry the hue of their light.
- **Cost (headless, SwiftShader, not representative):** the software keyframe bake takes about
  1.5 s at load for the three layers (in production it is a development-time script); a cache
  rebuild takes 1–50 ms. Real measurements need the dedicated monitor and the Surface Pro.
